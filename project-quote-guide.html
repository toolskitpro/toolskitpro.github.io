How to Quote a Project Without Undercharging

Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

"What will this cost?" — the question every freelancer dreads. Go too high and you lose the job. Go too low and you buy yourself weeks of underpaid work. The way out is not guessing better; it is quoting systematically.

Fixed Price or Hourly?

Each model protects you from a different risk:

  • Hourly protects you when the scope is fuzzy — research-heavy work, clients who change their mind, maintenance tasks. The risk: slow months mean low pay, and clients fear open-ended bills.
  • Fixed price rewards your efficiency — as you get faster, your effective hourly rate rises. The risk: scope creep quietly turns your $1,500 project into 80 hours of work.

The professional middle path: quote fixed price with a written scope, using your hourly rate as the internal calculation.

Step 1: Estimate Hours Honestly

Break the project into tasks — not "build website" but "homepage, 4 inner pages, contact form, revisions". Estimate each task, then add your real experience: most people underestimate by 30%. If your gut says 30 hours, plan for 40.

Step 2: Add Real Costs

Stock assets, premium plugins, paid fonts, outsourced help — list every dollar the project costs you before your time is counted.

Step 3: Add the Buffer (This Is Not Optional)

Every project has surprise work: extra revision rounds, misunderstood requirements, waiting on client materials. Add 15-25% to cover it. This buffer is not greed — it is the difference between freelancers who survive and freelancers who burn out.

Step 4: Present It Professionally

Send the quote as a short document: what you will deliver, the timeline, the price, and the payment terms (50% upfront is standard for fixed-price work). Round the number — $1,500 feels considered; $1,437 feels arbitrary.

Do the math for your next project with the project quote calculator — it adds the buffer for you so you do not talk yourself out of it.

What to Say When They Say "That Is Too Expensive"

Never apologize. Say: "I understand the budget is a concern. Here is what we can do within $X — we can cut these two parts and keep the rest at full quality." Reducing scope keeps your rate intact. Reducing your rate teaches the client to push harder.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much upfront payment should I ask for?

50% is the standard for fixed-price work. For projects under $300, 100% upfront is reasonable. For large projects, use milestones: 30% start, 40% midpoint, 30% delivery.

What is scope creep and how do I stop it?

Scope creep is unpaid extra work that just comes up. Stop it with a written scope in your quote and a simple sentence in your contract: work outside scope is quoted separately. Our contract guide shows how.

Should I match a competitor's lower price?

No. Clients choosing purely on price leave purely on price. Compete on clarity, reliability, and communication — those keep clients for years.

Is it okay to charge a rush fee?

Absolutely — urgent work disrupts your other commitments. 15-30% extra for deadlines that require overtime is standard practice, and clients who truly need speed pay it happily.
