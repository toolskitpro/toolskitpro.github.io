How to Price a Freelance Project

Practical guide for freelancers

Home › Guides › Project pricing

A project quote should do two jobs: help a client understand what they will pay, and help you decide whether the work is worth accepting. A number pulled from guesswork can leave out planning, communication, revisions, software, subcontractors, and the time needed to deliver the final work. A simple estimate makes those parts visible before you agree to the project.

1. Choose a pricing model that fits the work

Hourly pricing can work well when the request is ongoing, priorities may change, or the client needs flexible support. It is also useful when you cannot reasonably define every task at the start. Explain how time will be recorded, how often you will report it, and whether you need approval before exceeding a budget.

A fixed project price can suit work with clearly described deliverables, such as a defined set of web pages, a logo package, or a specific writing assignment. The client knows the agreed price for the agreed scope. You take more responsibility for estimating the effort, so define what is included and how extra requests will be handled. A fixed fee should not mean unlimited revisions or unlimited work.

Some freelancers combine the two: they offer a fixed price for the agreed deliverables and use an hourly rate to price any approved work outside that scope. The right model depends on uncertainty, client expectations, and how clearly the outcome can be described.

2. Write down the scope before estimating

Start with the outcome the client wants. Turn that outcome into a list of deliverables and tasks. For a small business website, that list might include a planning call, five page layouts, responsive checks, a contact form, one content pass, two revision rounds, and handover notes. Also list exclusions, such as writing all website copy, buying a domain, or creating extra pages.

Ask the client what they will supply and when. Missing copy, late feedback, unclear decision-making, or delayed access can affect the schedule. You do not need to predict every problem; you need to make the main assumptions clear so both sides know what the estimate relies on.

3. Estimate the hours by task

Estimate each task separately rather than assigning one number to the whole project. Include discovery, messages and meetings, research, production, quality checks, revisions included in the scope, and delivery. If similar past projects are available, use your own time records as a starting point. If you are new to this kind of work, mark the uncertain parts and ask questions before finalising the quote.

Do not add an arbitrary percentage just because a guide says everyone underestimates. Instead, identify the uncertain tasks. You might estimate a range internally, ask the client to clarify the requirements, or include a clearly explained contingency where the project genuinely needs it. A buffer is a planning choice, not permission to leave the scope vague.

4. Add project expenses and calculate a base amount

List costs that exist because of this specific project: paid stock assets, licensed fonts, specialist software, a subcontractor, printing, or travel. Decide whether the client pays these directly or reimburses you. Avoid counting the same cost twice. Your general business overhead and unpaid administrative time should already be reflected in the hourly rate you use to estimate the work, if that is how you set your rate.

A straightforward starting formula is: estimated hours × your internal hourly rate + project-specific costs. Next, add any clearly justified contingency and any rush charge that you have discussed with the client. State whether taxes, platform fees, or payment processing fees are included or billed separately. Their treatment depends on your location and business arrangements.

5. Worked example: a small website project

Suppose you estimate 30 hours for a defined website project and use an internal rate of $40 per hour. You also expect $100 in project-specific assets. For this illustration, assume you choose a 20% contingency on the time cost plus direct costs, and there is no rush fee. The percentage is an example for the arithmetic, not a universal recommendation.

  1. Time cost: 30 × $40 = $1,200.
  2. Add direct costs: $1,200 + $100 = $1,300.
  3. Calculate the example contingency: 20% × $1,300 = $260.
  4. Add it to the base: $1,300 + $260 = $1,560.

First, calculate the time cost: 30 hours × $40 = $1,200. Add the $100 in project costs to get a base of $1,300. A 20% contingency on $1,300 is $260. Add that amount to the base: $1,300 + $260 = $1,560. The illustrative quote is $1,560 before any applicable tax or separately agreed charges.

Before sending the number, check that the 30-hour estimate includes planning, client communication, testing, handover, and the revisions promised in the scope. If any of those are missing, update the estimate rather than relying on the contingency to cover everything. Use the free project quote calculator to work through the arithmetic.

6. Present the quote so the client can make a decision

A useful quote is more than a total. Include the project name, date, deliverables, exclusions, schedule, number of included revision rounds, client responsibilities, price, payment schedule, quote expiry date if applicable, and how additional work will be approved. Use plain language. If you are making assumptions—for example, that the client supplies final copy—write them down.

Avoid presenting a contingency as a hidden fee. If you include a risk allowance in a fixed price, explain the assumptions and scope that price covers. If a project is too uncertain to price responsibly, offer a paid discovery phase or a time-and-materials arrangement until the scope is clearer.

7. Agree payment milestones and change requests

There is no single deposit percentage that suits every freelancer, client, country, or project. Depending on the work and local rules, you might agree an initial deposit, payments at defined milestones, or payment on completion. For larger projects, milestones can reduce the amount either side has at risk at one time. Put dates or clear triggers in writing, and state when the final files or handover will be delivered.

If a client requests something outside the agreed scope, pause before doing the extra work. Describe the change, its effect on price and timing, and ask for written approval. This protects the relationship by replacing surprise charges with a clear decision. Your freelance contract guide can help you think through the written terms.

Common pricing mistakes to avoid

  • Leaving out planning, communication, testing, and handover time.
  • Using a vague scope without exclusions or revision limits.
  • Treating a contingency as a replacement for clear requirements.
  • Forgetting project-specific costs or counting them twice.
  • Promising a deadline before confirming client inputs and approvals.
  • Starting extra work before agreeing its price and schedule.

Frequently asked questions

How much deposit should I request?

There is no universal deposit amount. Choose terms that fit the project size, cash-flow needs, client relationship, and applicable local rules. Agree the amount and timing in writing before work starts.

What contingency percentage should I use?

There is no percentage that fits every project. Base any allowance on the uncertainties you can identify, explain the assumptions, and clarify what happens if the scope changes.

Should I charge hourly or use a fixed price?

Hourly pricing can fit evolving or ongoing work. A fixed price can fit a well-defined scope. You can also quote a fixed set of deliverables and price approved extra work separately.

What if the client says the quote is too expensive?

Ask which part of the budget is difficult. Offer a smaller scope or phased delivery rather than quietly removing essential work from the same price.

Does this quote calculator add tax?

No. The calculator estimates a project quote from time, costs, buffer, and rush fee inputs. Tax rules depend on your location and circumstances, so verify them separately.
