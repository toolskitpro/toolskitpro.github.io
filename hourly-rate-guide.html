How to Calculate Your Freelance Hourly Rate

Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Freelancers around the world price their work in different currencies and operate under different tax systems, but the basic pricing problem is similar: your rate must cover the income you want, business costs, unpaid working time and a margin for risk. This guide gives you a practical planning formula you can adapt to your own market.

Why Guessing Your Rate Always Ends Badly

Freelancers need to allow for software, equipment, insurance where relevant, training, holidays, sick days, administration and time spent finding clients. A salary divided by a standard full-time work year can understate the rate you need because not every working hour can be invoiced.

The Real Formula

Your hourly rate has to cover four things at once:

  1. Your target income — what you want to actually keep per year.
  2. Your business expenses — software, equipment, internet, courses, insurance.
  3. Your tax provision — use an informed estimate based on your country, income and circumstances. Tax rates differ widely, so do not treat one percentage as universal.
  4. Your unbillable time — and this is the big one.

Your billable percentage depends on your work and business. Time spent emailing, invoicing, marketing, learning, managing projects and handling admin is necessary but often not directly billable. Start with a realistic estimate, then adjust it using your time records after a few weeks.

So the formula becomes:

Hourly rate = (target income + expenses) ÷ (1 - tax rate) ÷ (working weeks × hours per week × billable %)

Important: This is a simplified planning formula. Use a tax assumption that reflects your own situation, keep all figures in one currency, and check whether local rules or business costs require a different approach.

A Worked Example

For a currency-neutral example, suppose your target personal income is 50,000 units of your chosen currency, annual business expenses are 5,000 units, your estimated tax provision is 25%, you take 4 weeks off, work 40 hours a week and bill 60% of your working time. These are illustrative assumptions, not a recommendation for any particular country.

  • Billable hours: 48 weeks × 40 hours × 60% = 1,152 hours
  • Gross needed: (50,000 + 5,000) ÷ 0.75 = about 73,333 currency units
  • Minimum rate: 73,333 ÷ 1,152 = about 63.66 currency units per hour

The important takeaway is the method, not the currency symbol: compare your calculated floor with market rates and the value you deliver, then refine your assumptions with real business data.

Add a Margin on Top

The number you calculated is your floor — the rate below which you lose money. Consider adding a margin that reflects profit goals, uncertainty, negotiation room and the value you deliver. There is no universal percentage: test your price against demand, comparable services and your positioning. For example, if your floor is 63.66 currency units, you might test a quote around 75 units when the market and scope support it.

Make the Rate Work for Your Market

  • Choose one currency and use it consistently for income, expenses and the result; this tool does not perform currency conversion.
  • Use a realistic tax percentage for your own circumstances. The calculator does not know your local tax rules or personal allowances.
  • Check competitor pricing, client budgets and the value of your service in your target market.
  • Review the rate when costs, demand, skills or billable utilisation change.

Turn Your Minimum Rate Into a Client Quote

Your calculated rate is a planning baseline, not necessarily the price you should show every client. Before sending a quote, define the outcome, deliverables, timeline, number of revision rounds and what is outside scope. Estimate the hours, multiply by your target rate, then add any direct costs and a suitable allowance for uncertainty.

  • For hourly work: state the rate, how time is recorded, billing increments and whether you set a spending cap.
  • For fixed-price work: estimate effort using your hourly floor, then document the deliverables, exclusions and change-request process.
  • For retainers: define the hours or outputs included, response times, rollover policy and how extra work is billed.

Keep the currency consistent throughout the quote and make clear whether taxes, platform charges, travel or other expenses are included. Rules and tax treatment vary by jurisdiction, so check local requirements before issuing a formal quote.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I turn an hourly rate into a day rate?

Multiply your hourly rate by the number of hours you plan to sell in a working day. For example, a six-hour billable day at 60 currency units per hour gives a 360-unit day rate. State what the day includes, how breaks are handled, and what happens if the scope changes.

Should I publish my hourly rate on my website?

It depends on your service and sales process. Publishing a starting rate can qualify enquiries and set expectations, while a tailored quote may fit work with different levels of complexity. If you publish a rate, explain what is included and whether taxes or third-party costs are extra.

What should I do if a client cannot afford my rate?

Do not immediately discount the same scope. Offer a smaller deliverable, fewer revisions, a phased project or a different timeline. Keep the agreed scope and payment terms in writing so a lower budget does not quietly become unpaid extra work.

Should I charge clients in my own currency or theirs?

Choose a clear policy and put the currency on every quote and invoice. If you accept a client's currency, account for exchange-rate movement and payment-provider fees in your pricing. This calculator does not convert currencies, so calculate with one consistent currency at a time.

When should I review my freelance rate?

Review it when your expenses, workload, expertise, demand or the type of clients you serve changes. Also compare your estimate with actual time records: if projects repeatedly take longer than expected, adjust your scope, process or price rather than absorbing the difference indefinitely.
